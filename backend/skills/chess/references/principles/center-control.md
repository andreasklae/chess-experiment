# Center control

## When to use
The opening and early middlegame — whenever the assessment says the opponent
controls the centre, or you are choosing where to put a pawn or develop a piece.
**Not an endgame concern:** with few pieces, king activity and passed pawns
decide, not the centre.

## The idea
The four central squares are **d4, e4, d5, e5**. A piece in or bearing on the
centre reaches both wings quickly and denies the opponent space; a side with the
centre can switch the attack from one side to the other faster than the defender
can respond. "Control" means **occupying** these squares (a pawn there is the
strongest claim) and **attacking** them with pawns and pieces. The whole-board
goal of the opening is to fight for this control while developing.

Two legitimate ways to do it:
- **Classical:** put pawns in the centre (1.e4, 1.d4, and the c-/d-/e-pawns) and
  back them with pieces. Direct, grabs space.
- **Hypermodern:** let the opponent build a big pawn centre, then attack it from
  the flanks with pieces (fianchettoed bishops, knights) and pawn breaks (c5,
  f5) so it becomes a target, not a strength.

## What to do
- In the opening, claim a central square with a pawn when you can (e4/d4), and
  aim knights at the centre (Nf3, Nc3 / Nf6, Nc6) — they support and attack
  d4/e4/d5/e5.
- If the opponent has the centre and you are cramped, **strike at it**: a pawn
  break (…c5, …f5, c4, f4) or piling pieces on a central pawn to win or dissolve
  it. Don't just shuffle — a free enemy centre grows.
- Support your own centre so a single break doesn't collapse it.

## Watch out for
- An over-extended centre is a **target**: pawns you can't defend get
  surrounded. Space is only good if you can hold it.
- Don't grab a wing pawn or chase material with the centre still contested and
  your pieces at home — you fall behind in development.
- Control is a means, not the move: the assessment names the idea; **calculate
  the concrete pawn break or developing move yourself** before playing it.

## Examples
- After `1.e4 a6 2.d4`, White has pawns on e4+d4 and Black has nothing central —
  White has a free classical centre and more space; Black must break with …c5
  or …e5 soon. FEN: `rnbqkbnr/1ppppppp/p7/8/3PP3/8/PPP2PPP/RNBQKBNR b KQkq - 0 2`.
- After `1.e4 e5 2.Nf3`, the centre is contested and balanced — neither side
  dominates; keep developing.
