# Opera mate

## When to use

The enemy king is still in the centre of its back rank (uncastled or
half-developed), a central file is open for your rook, and your bishop can
reach the diagonal that crosses the rook's landing square. The classic
punishment for a king left on e8/d8 too long.

## The idea

Back-rank geometry without the castled-corner: the rook lands **next to**
the uncastled king on the back rank, and a bishop protects the rook from
afar along the diagonal (g5–d8 type). The king's own undeveloped pieces —
knight on b8, bishop on f8, the pawn shield — are the walls. It is
[[mates/back-rank-mate]] where a bishop substitutes for
the second guard.

## What to do

1. Open the central file the enemy king lives on (trades or a sacrifice on
   the blocking pawn/piece — Morphy gave a queen for it).
2. Put the bishop on the diagonal that defends the mating square (g5
   defends d8 through f6–e7).
3. Rook to the mating square with check. The king cannot take (bishop),
   cannot step up (bishop covers e7), and its own pieces block the rest.

## Watch out for

- The diagonal must be clear the whole way — a single interposable defender
  (...Nd7, ...Be7) refutes it; check the "Opponent legal replies" table.
- If the king can step to the second rank, you have a strong check, not
  mate — drive it back or cover that square first.

## Examples

`1n1Rkb1r/p4ppp/4q3/4p1B1/4P3/8/PPP2PPP/2K5 b - - 0 1` — the Opera Game
final picture (Morphy, Paris 1858): Rd8 mates Ke8, protected by Bg5 through
f6–e7; Nb8, Bf8 and the pawns are Black's own walls. Verified.
