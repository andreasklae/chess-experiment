# London: meeting ...Qb6 (the loose b2-pawn)

## When to use
Black has played **...Qb6**, attacking your **b2-pawn** (loose now that your bishop
left c1) and usually also pressing **d4** with **...c5**. This is the London's main
test — handle it precisely; do NOT just leave b2 hanging.

## The idea
The right answer **depends on whether you have already played c3**, because **Qb3
(offering a queen trade) is only legal once c3 has opened the d1–b3 diagonal**. Picking
the wrong one loses the b2-pawn for nothing.

## What to do
**Case A — you have ALREADY played c3** (e.g. 1.d4 d5 2.Bf4 Nf6 3.e3 c5 4.c3 Qb6):
- Play **Qb3** — offer the queen trade. After **...Qxb3 axb3** your structure is fine
  (the open a-file and doubled b-pawns are harmless; b2 is no longer a target). This is
  the cleanest equaliser.
- If Black avoids the trade, your queen on b3 still defends b2 and eyes b7/d5.

**Case B — you have NOT yet played c3** (e.g. 1.d4 d5 2.Bf4 c5 3.e3 Qb6 — Qb3 is
ILLEGAL here, the queen is blocked):
- **b3** — simply defend the pawn. Solid; after b3 ...Qxb2?? is impossible. Slightly
  passive but safe.
- **Qc1** — defends b2 and keeps the option of redeveloping the queen later. Note b2 is
  guarded but still pressured, so don't drift.
- **Nc3** (a gambit) — ignore b2 and develop fast; if Black grabs **...Qxb2** you get
  a big lead in development and open lines (Na4 traps the queen ideas). Only play this
  if you want a sharp game and have checked the line.

**Case C — a sharper try: give up b2 for development.** Two forms, and note the
difference (verified):
- **...c5-early, before c3** (1.d4 Nf6 2.Bf4 c5 3.e3 Qb6 4.Nc3): if **...Qxb2** then
  **5.Nb5!** hits c7/a7 and the queen — a real gambit with the lead in development.
- **dxc5 gambit** (after ...c5 ...Qb6): **dxc5!** grabs the pawn; if **...Qxb2**, DON'T
  reach for Rb1 (your b1-knight is still home, so **Rb1 is illegal**) — play **Nd2**,
  developing while the b2-queen sits awkwardly and you keep the extra c5-pawn.

## Qb3 is a MOVE-1-of-the-...Qb6 answer, not forever
**Qb3 is right only right after ...Qb6, while the position is still standard.** If the
queen has sat on b6 for several moves and the position has developed (pieces out, files
opening, a tactic in the air), **Qb3 is often now a positional blunder** — you'd be
sending the queen offside for no reason. In that case forget the "book" Qb3: pick the
best move by normal judgement (finish development, meet a threat, or take a tactic).
The rule is *answer ...Qb6 when it arrives*, not *play Qb3 whenever a queen is on b6*.

## Watch out for — traps
- **Qc2 then ...Bf5 loses a pawn:** after Qc2, Black plays **...Bf5!**; **Qxf5?? Qxb2**
  wins for Black. So don't put the queen on c2 in front of the ...Bf5 pin without care.
- **Miles–Minasian move order:** 1.d4 d5 2.Bf4 c5 3.e3 Nc6 4.c3 Qb6 5.Qb3 c4 6.Qc2 Bf5
  7.Qxf5! and now **...Qxb2?? loses to 8.Qxd5** — move order matters, calculate it.
- **Don't reflexively play Qb3 — verify it is LEGAL first** (needs c3 in; else b3/Qc1).
- ...Qb6 often comes with **...c4** (clamping) or **...Bf5** — meet ...Bf5 by developing
  (Nbd2, Be2/Bd3), not by chasing it.

## Examples (verified)
- Case A (c3 in): `1.d4 d5 2.Bf4 Nf6 3.e3 c5 4.c3 Qb6 5.Qb3` — legal, offers the trade.
- Case B (no c3): `1.d4 d5 2.Bf4 c5 3.e3 Qb6` — **Qb3 illegal**; play **b3** or **Qc1**.
