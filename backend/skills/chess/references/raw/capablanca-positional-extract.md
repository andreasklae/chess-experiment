# Capablanca — *Chess Fundamentals* (1921): positional & strategic principles digest

> **This is a paraphrased digest, not the source.** The verbatim public-domain
> full text is in `capablanca-chess-fundamentals-GUTENBERG-33870.txt` (Project
> Gutenberg #33870) and the original scanned `Chess Fundamentals_.pdf`. This file
> digests the **positional and middlegame/opening strategy** prose that the other
> Capablanca digest (`chess-fundamentals-capablanca.md`) omitted (it focused on
> mates and endgame technique). See `SOURCES.md`.

Descriptive notation in the original (P-K4, Kt-KB3) is paraphrased into
principles here; concrete lines are not replayed because the value of these
chapters is the *reasoning*, not specific variations. Page references are to the
PDF.

## Piece values and how they change (Ch. III, §14, p21)

- A bishop is often worth more than three pawns; a knight seldom so.
- A rook is worth a knight + two pawns, or a bishop + two pawns (the bishop is
  the better piece against the rook).
- Two rooks are slightly stronger than a queen.
- **The power of the knight DECREASES as pieces are exchanged off; the power of
  the rook INCREASES.** (Key practical consequence: rooks and queens gain value
  as the board empties — relevant to what to keep when trading.)
- The king is a defensive piece in the middlegame but becomes an **offensive**
  piece in the endgame; its handling is paramount once few pieces remain.

## The initiative (Ch. IV, §20, p63)

- Both sides start equal in material and position; White has the move, and the
  move *is* the initiative — an advantage.
- **Keep the initiative as long as possible; only give it up for a material or
  positional advantage in return.** Develop fast, hinder the opponent's
  development, apply pressure, fight for the centre.

## General strategy of the opening (Ch. III, §6, p21)

- **The main thing is to develop the pieces quickly — get them into play as
  fast as you can.**
- 1.e4 or 1.d4 open lines for the queen and a bishop; theoretically one of
  these must be best because no other first move accomplishes as much.
- Knight to f3 (or f6) is both an attacking and a developing move.
- **It is generally advisable not to bring a bishop out until one knight is
  out, preferably the king's knight.** (Knights before bishops.)

## Control of the centre (Ch. III, §7, p23)

- The four central squares (e4, d4, e5, d5) are the centre. **No violent attack
  can succeed without controlling at least two, possibly three, of them.**
- Control of the centre invariably ensures the initiative. Many opening
  manoeuvres exist solely to win the centre.
- **In the opening, whenever possible, move pieces in preference to pawns.** A
  timid early pawn move (assuming a defensive posture) is, on principle, wrong.

## Opening mistakes to avoid (Ch. III, §7-8, p24)

- **Develop at least one knight before bringing the bishops out.**
- **Exchanging a bishop for a knight in the opening is generally bad** unless
  there is compensation.
- **Do not bring the queen out early** — having only the queen developed while
  the opponent has a bishop and knight out gives the opponent a quick advantage.
- Beginners overuse early rook-pawn moves (a3, h3 / a6, h6) — avoid them.

## Salient points about pawns (Ch. V, §31, p107-108)

- **Pawn-structure weaknesses, once created, are usually permanent** — so they
  must be avoided.
- A **backward pawn** on a (half-)open file is a weak point the opponent can
  pile up against down the file.
- A **"hole"** is a square in front of/around your pawns that your pawns can no
  longer control (e.g. after the f/g/h pawns advance, the square they vacated).
  A piece established on a hole "could not be dislodged" without exchanging it,
  which is often inconvenient. **Such formations invariably lead to disaster.**
- **Two or more pawns are strongest when side by side on the same rank**
  (connected, abreast). Centre pawns are strongest abreast on the 4th rank;
  advancing one to the 5th is a committal decision that often determines the
  game's course.
- **A passed pawn is either very weak or very strong**, and its
  strength/weakness *increases as it advances* and is in direct relation to the
  number of pieces on the board: **a passed pawn grows stronger as pieces come
  off** (toward the endgame).

## Attacking on both wings — greater mobility (Ch. IV, §26, p87)

- A way to win: **attack first on one side, then — given greater mobility —
  transfer the attack quickly to the other side, breaking through before the
  opponent can bring up enough defenders.** Fix the opponent's pawns to create
  fixed targets, tie down his pieces, then switch fronts. This middlegame
  principle also applies in rook endings.

## Coordination (Ch. IV, p63)

- Ideal middlegame play: when the opportunity is offered, all the pieces are
  thrown into action *en masse*, coordinating "with machine-like precision."
